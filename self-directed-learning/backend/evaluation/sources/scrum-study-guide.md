02 Scrum foundations and empiricism
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 5, 8-11, 56-58. This guide is a selective educational
paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Scrum: a compact definition
Scrum is a lightweight framework for teams tackling complex work. Rather than attempting to predict
every detail of a product upfront, the team creates useful results in short cycles, learns from what
happens, and adapts what it does next.
Why an iterative framework helps
Software requirements evolve. Users discover needs after interacting with a working feature;
unexpected technical issues arise; estimates may be wrong. Scrum responds to uncertainty by keeping
plans inspectable and making regular adjustments possible. A Sprint provides a recurring timebox for
producing a usable Increment and learning from it.
Empiricism: three pillars
 • Transparency: important facts about work and product quality should be visible and understandable.
 A shared backlog or a clear Definition of Done can help create a common view.
• Inspection: the team and stakeholders examine progress, results, and current assumptions frequently
 enough to detect problems or new opportunities.
• Adaptation: when evidence shows that something should change, the team adjusts the plan, process,
 or product direction in a timely way.
Example: course registration application
A student team assumes learners want a long registration form. During an early review, students report
that mobile signup is frustrating. The team inspects that feedback and reorders the backlog to make
signup simpler. This illustrates learning through actual product evidence rather than relying solely on an
initial plan.
Self-management and cross-functional capability
A Scrum Team decides internally who works on which tasks, when, and how. Team members collectively
need the skills required to create useful results. That does not imply everyone has identical expertise;
collaboration can span design, programming, testing, analysis, and user experience.
Distinction to remember
Scrum provides a framework of accountabilities, artifacts, commitments, and events. It is not a detailed
checklist for every software development activity.

04 Scrum Team and accountabilities
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 12-14, 19-23, 28-31. This guide is a selective
educational paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
One team, three accountabilities
The Scrum Team is a small, self-managing, cross-functional unit. The Product Owner, Scrum Master, and
Developers have different areas of accountability but cooperate toward a shared Product Goal. These
terms describe accountabilities, not a rigid hierarchy of job titles.
Product Owner
The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value and for effective Product Backlog
management. That includes communicating the Product Goal, creating or clearly expressing backlog
items, ordering them, and ensuring the backlog is transparent and understandable. Work can be
delegated, but the accountability remains with the Product Owner.
Scrum Master
The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as understood by the team and for helping the
Scrum Team improve its effectiveness. This includes coaching self-management, helping remove
impediments, and supporting productive interactions with stakeholders. A Scrum Master is not
automatically the project boss or the person assigning every programming task.
Developers
Developers are the Scrum Team members accountable for creating the usable Increment. They plan
how to meet the Sprint Goal, adapt the Sprint Backlog as work progresses, maintain quality, and hold
one another accountable for professional teamwork. The term includes people contributing relevant
skills, not just code authors.
Example responsibilities in a university project
 • Product Owner: clarify whether students most need search, event details, or registration, and order
 backlog items accordingly.
• Scrum Master: facilitate collaboration when a team is blocked by unclear responsibilities or recurring
 coordination problems.
• Developers: design, implement, test, and integrate a working event-registration feature.
 
Common misconception
A daily task board may show individual assignments, but Scrum does not make the Product Owner or
Scrum Master the mandatory dispatcher of all technical work.

05 Collaboration, stakeholders, and team
pitfalls
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 14-19, 22-24, 27-31. This guide is a selective
educational paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Cooperation is more important than role silos
Product understanding and engineering knowledge must circulate across the team. The Product Owner
brings product priorities and stakeholder context. Developers bring implementation constraints and
technical insights. The Scrum Master helps the group collaborate effectively and use empiricism rather
than hierarchy to decide its next move.
Stakeholders
Stakeholders may be customers, users, sponsors, instructors, or support staff whose needs and
feedback influence the product. They are not automatically members of the Scrum Team. In a Sprint
Review, their participation can help reveal whether the product is moving in a valuable direction.
Three recurring failure patterns
 • Over-control: a leader dictates solutions and checks every small action; Developers lose ownership
 and problems get hidden.
• Unclear priorities: the team starts many features without a shared Product Goal or understanding of
 relative value.
• Fragmented collaboration: individuals optimize their own tasks while integration, testing, or help for
 blocked teammates is neglected.
Healthier responses
Use a visible backlog, explain why items matter, make impediments discussable, and inspect working
results. Encourage Developers to propose plans and trade-offs. When priorities change, discuss the
impact explicitly rather than silently pushing extra tasks onto the team.
Student team exercise
A four-person team has one designer, two programmers, and one tester. A key API is failing, but each
person insists on completing only their original tasks. How might self-management and cross-functional
collaboration change what the team does next?
Scope note
The source ebook devotes many pages to detailed personality traits, conflicts, and organizational
implementation problems. This condensed guide retains the practical teaching themes rather than
reproducing those exhaustive lists.

06 Scrum artifacts: three sources of
transparency
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 5-7, 34-38, 52-53. This guide is a selective
educational paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Product Backlog
The Product Backlog is an emergent, ordered list of work needed to improve a product. It is the single
source of work undertaken by the Scrum Team. Its contents can include desired features, changes,
defects, and exploratory tasks. Items evolve as the team learns more about users, technology, and
priorities.
Sprint Backlog
The Sprint Backlog contains the Sprint Goal, the Product Backlog items selected for that Sprint, and a
plan for delivering them. Developers create and adapt this plan. It is a living representation of how the
team intends to achieve the Sprint Goal, not a promise that every original task description can never
change.
Increment
An Increment is a concrete step toward the Product Goal that meets the Definition of Done. Multiple
Increments may be created within a Sprint. A feature that has been coded but not integrated or
validated against the agreed quality standard should not be presented as a completed Increment.
Example: campus event registration
 • Product Backlog item: allow a student to register for an available event.
 • Sprint Backlog plan: implement a registration endpoint, add eligibility checks, build the registration
 interface, write tests, and integrate with the event page.
• Increment: a working, tested registration flow that meets the team’s Definition of Done.
 
One-sentence distinction
Product Backlog = evolving possibilities and priorities; Sprint Backlog = this Sprint’s goal and working
plan; Increment = useful product work demonstrably complete to the agreed standard.

07 Commitments: goals and Definition of Done
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 7, 53, 58-60. This guide is a selective educational
paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Scrum associates each artifact with a commitment. The commitments help focus the team and make
progress and quality easier to inspect.
Product Goal → Product Backlog
The Product Goal is a long-term objective describing the future state of the product that the Scrum
Team seeks. The rest of the Product Backlog defines emerging work toward it. Example: enable
students to find and register for campus events without requiring manual staff assistance.
Sprint Goal → Sprint Backlog
The Sprint Goal is a single objective for the Sprint. It gives coherence and flexibility to the Sprint plan.
Example: make it possible for a student to complete a reliable event registration from a phone.
Definition of Done → Increment
The Definition of Done is a shared, explicit description of the quality conditions that work must satisfy
before it can count as part of an Increment. This helps prevent different people from using incompatible
meanings of “done.”
Example Definition of Done for a student application
 • Code has been reviewed and merged into the integration branch.
 • Relevant automated tests pass; core user path has been manually verified.
 • Known severe defects affecting this feature are resolved.
 • Feature is integrated with the application and can be demonstrated.
 • User-facing behavior and limitations are documented appropriately.
 These example checks are team-selected engineering conditions, not a universal list required by Scrum.
Acceptance criteria are different
Acceptance criteria describe the behavior expected for a particular backlog item or user story. The
Definition of Done is the wider quality standard applied to work within the Increment. A story may meet
its behavioral examples and still fail the Definition of Done if tests are failing or integration is
incomplete.

10 Sprint and Sprint Planning
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 54-59. This guide is a selective educational
paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
What a Sprint is
A Sprint is a fixed-length event of one month or less in which ideas are turned into value. A new Sprint
begins immediately after the previous one. The Sprint contains the other Scrum events and the work
needed to create one or more usable Increments.
Sprint Planning: three questions
 • Why is this Sprint valuable? The team establishes a Sprint Goal that expresses the value of the Sprint.
 • What can be Done this Sprint? Developers select Product Backlog items through discussion with the
 Product Owner and consideration of capacity and past performance.
• How will the chosen work get Done? Developers plan the work needed to produce an Increment that
 meets the Definition of Done.
A planning example
The student project team chooses a two-week Sprint Goal: “Students can browse events and register for
one event.” They select the event listing and registration backlog items. Developers identify an API,
front-end work, database changes, validation rules, and tests. They discuss what functionality can be
simplified if integration takes longer than expected.
Changes during a Sprint
A Sprint is not intended to freeze learning. Work and scope can be clarified and renegotiated as more is
learned. The Sprint Goal should not be jeopardized, quality should not decrease, and refinement
continues. A plan is inspected and adapted instead of treated as an immutable contract.
Common planning failure
Do not build a Sprint Backlog by merely dividing all requested tasks among people without agreeing on
the Sprint Goal, available capacity, and what completing the feature would mean.

11 Daily Scrum and Sprint Review
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 6, 54-56, 70-73. This guide is a selective
educational paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Daily Scrum
The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and
adapt the Sprint Backlog as necessary. The familiar prompts “What did I do yesterday?”, “What will I do
today?”, and “What is blocking me?” can help, but they are optional. The event should be about
coordinating next steps, not reporting status to a manager.
Example Daily Scrum conversation
“Our event listing now works, but registration fails for already-full events. To protect the Sprint Goal,
two of us will pair on capacity validation while the third tests the student flow. We will update the Sprint
Backlog after this meeting.”
Sprint Review
In the Sprint Review, the Scrum Team and relevant stakeholders inspect the result of the Sprint and
discuss progress toward the Product Goal. They consider what has changed and collaborate on what to
do next. It is a working session around real product evidence, not just a slide presentation.
Example review
The team demonstrates event registration to student representatives. Test users request an obvious
confirmation message and a clearer full-event status. The Product Owner discusses their value and
updates or reorders backlog items. The review may affect future work without implying every
suggestion must be accepted.
Daily Scrum versus Sprint Review
 • Daily Scrum: Developers coordinate how to progress toward the current Sprint Goal.
 • Sprint Review: the team and stakeholders inspect product results, learn, and adapt future product
 direction.
Check your understanding
Why would a Daily Scrum that consists only of reading individual task percentages miss part of its
purpose? Why is a Sprint Review stronger when stakeholders can interact with working software?

12 Retrospectives, metrics, and Kanban
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 25-26, 54-55, 60-68, 74. This guide is a selective
educational paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Sprint Retrospective
The Sprint Retrospective inspects how the last Sprint went in terms of people, interactions, processes,
tools, and Definition of Done. The Scrum Team identifies improvements and can add meaningful
improvement work to the next Sprint. It is different from Sprint Review because it focuses on the team’s
way of working rather than primarily on the product outcome.
Example retrospective
The team notices that most pull requests remained unreviewed until the last day. It agrees to review
small changes daily and to keep the integration branch deployable. In the next Sprint, the team checks
whether review delays actually decreased.
Optional measurement and visualization tools
 • Burndown chart: shows an estimate of work remaining over time; useful but dependent on consistent
 measurement and realistic interpretations.
• Burnup chart: shows cumulative progress against an amount of work, which can make scope changes
 easier to notice.
• Cumulative flow diagram: can illustrate how work accumulates in stages, potentially exposing
 bottlenecks.
• Velocity: a team-specific historical indicator based on its chosen estimation unit; not a dependable
 comparison between different teams.
Kanban boards and Scrumban
A Kanban-style board visualizes stages such as To Do, In Progress, Review, and Done. Boards can help a
Scrum Team see workflow, but Kanban is not identical to Scrum. The ebook also discusses Scrumban, a
hybrid that borrows selected practices from both. These are complementary and alternative workflow
choices, not mandatory Scrum elements.
Metric caution
Measures can guide discussion, but pressure to improve a number can distort behavior. Inspect working
software, quality, and customer outcomes rather than assuming a chart alone proves that the team is
performing well.

13 End-to-end worked case study
SOURCE MAP: Firmbee, Basics of Scrum (2023), original PDF pages 40-47, 52-59, 70-74. This guide is a selective
educational paraphrase, not a complete reproduction.
Project: campus event registration
Product Goal: students can discover and register for university events quickly, with reliable availability
information.
Initial Product Backlog, ordered by expected value
 • PBI-1: Browse upcoming events with date, location, and title.
 • PBI-2: Register for an event that has free capacity.
 • PBI-3: Receive a visible registration confirmation.
 • PBI-4: Cancel a registration.
 • PBI-5: Filter events by category.
 
Example user story and acceptance criteria
Story: As a student, I want to register for an event, so that I can reserve my place without contacting the
organizer.
 • A student may register only when capacity remains.
 • Submitting a valid registration increases the registration count once.
 • A full event displays a clear explanation and does not accept the registration.
 
Sprint 1 plan
Sprint Goal: a student can find an event and successfully register. Selected items: PBI-1 and PBI-2; PBI-3
if capacity allows. Developer plan: create event data structures, listing UI, registration API, integration
tests, and an end-to-end demonstration.
Inspect and adapt
At the Daily Scrum, the team discovers duplicate submissions can create double registrations. It adapts
the plan to validate duplicate requests. At Sprint Review, students ask for a prominent confirmation
state. In Retrospective, the team decides to add a review checklist for boundary cases such as full
events and duplicate submissions.
What counts as the Increment?
Only tested, integrated behavior that meets the shared Definition of Done. An unfinished filter or a
mocked registration button is not labelled complete simply because a UI screenshot exists.
